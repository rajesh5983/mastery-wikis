## Ship Publicly

**Status:** ⬜ Not Started

**Definition:** Shipping publicly means releasing actual working AI tools, demos, or applications to real users — not just writing about ideas. Public projects create a portfolio of evidence for your skills, attract community feedback that accelerates learning, and build reputation as someone who executes rather than just theorises.

**Mental Model:** Shipping publicly is like publishing a paper vs. just having the idea. The idea has zero verifiable value to anyone outside your head; the published work has compounding value — it can be found, cited, forked, and built upon.

**Common Misconceptions:**
- Projects must be polished and complete before sharing — a functional rough demo shared early gets feedback that makes the final version far better than a polished solo project no one sees until launch.
- Shipping publicly requires significant infrastructure — a Hugging Face Space, a GitHub repo, or a simple hosted demo costs nothing to ship and immediately validates that something works.

**Interview Skeleton:**
- What it is: the practice of releasing working AI projects to the public to build portfolio evidence and accelerate learning through feedback
- Why it matters: public shipped work is the strongest possible evidence of AI engineering competence; it compounds over time through discovered users and network effects
- Example: describe a small AI project you'd ship publicly in a weekend that would demonstrate a specific AI engineering skill to a hiring manager

## Open Source Contribution

**Status:** ⬜ Not Started

**Definition:** Contributing to open source AI projects (LangChain, LlamaIndex, Hugging Face Transformers, dbt, Apache Airflow) builds deep understanding of production-grade codebases, creates public evidence of technical quality, and builds relationships with other engineers in the ecosystem.

**Mental Model:** Open source contribution is like doing an internship with the most impactful AI projects in the world — except you choose the project, the task, and the schedule, and all the work is permanently publicly visible.

**Common Misconceptions:**
- You must be expert-level to contribute — documentation improvements, bug reports with reproducible examples, and small bug fixes are genuinely valued contributions for any experience level.
- Contributions must be large features to matter — consistent small, high-quality contributions build more reputation and relationships than one large contribution that disappears into inactivity.

**Interview Skeleton:**
- What it is: contributing code, documentation, or issues to open source AI projects to build skills, relationships, and public track record
- Why it matters: open source contributions are publicly verifiable evidence of technical ability and collaborative work style
- Example: identify a specific, manageable contribution you could make to an AI engineering open source project this week

**Free Resources:** https://eugeneyan.com — Eugene Yan's writing on open source contribution strategies and career growth in AI engineering

## Writing

**Status:** ⬜ Not Started

**Definition:** Writing technical content — blog posts, tutorials, documentation, LinkedIn posts, newsletters — about what you build and learn creates a durable public record of your thinking, attracts like-minded engineers to your network, and forces the deep understanding that comes from explaining something well to someone else.

**Mental Model:** Writing is thinking made durable. A lesson you document in a blog post is available to everyone forever. A lesson you keep in your head is lost when you change jobs.

**Common Misconceptions:**
- You can only write about things you fully master — writing to learn is a powerful technique; "here is what I understand so far and where I'm confused" posts are highly valuable to readers at the same stage.
- Writing takes too long to be worth the time — a 500-word explanation of one specific technique you learned this week takes one hour and compounds indefinitely in search traffic and reputation.

**Interview Skeleton:**
- What it is: publishing technical content that demonstrates understanding, attracts opportunities, and builds a compounding public record
- Why it matters: strong technical writers are rare in engineering; writing creates an asymmetric career advantage over engineers with equivalent technical skills who don't publish
- Example: identify one concept from your recent learning that you could explain clearly in a 400-word post this week

**Free Resources:** https://eugeneyan.com — Eugene Yan's blog demonstrating the career impact of consistent technical writing in AI engineering

## Communities

**Status:** ⬜ Not Started

**Definition:** Participating in AI engineering communities — Discord servers, Slack groups, Twitter/X, local meetups, and conferences — provides early access to new techniques, peer learning, mentorship, and hiring opportunities. The AI field moves fast enough that community signals often precede mainstream adoption by 6–12 months.

**Mental Model:** Communities are intelligence networks — you learn what's actually working in production (not just in papers) from people who are building the same things you are, often before it's written up anywhere.

**Common Misconceptions:**
- Community participation is networking for introverts — the primary value is learning and problem-solving alongside peers, not schmoozing; passive reading and occasional thoughtful comments build presence over time.
- Online communities are not as valuable as professional networks — the AI engineering community is largely online-first; some of the most influential practitioners are reachable on Twitter/Discord, not at traditional networking events.

**Interview Skeleton:**
- What it is: active participation in communities where AI engineers share what they're building, learning, and struggling with
- Why it matters: community membership provides access to practical knowledge that lags 12–18 months behind when it appears in mainstream articles
- Example: name three specific communities (Discord servers, newsletters, or Slack groups) relevant to AI engineering and describe what you'd contribute to each

## Build in Public

**Status:** ⬜ Not Started

**Definition:** Building in public means sharing your progress, failures, learnings, and decisions openly as you build — through Twitter threads, LinkedIn posts, YouTube devlogs, or public GitHub commits. This creates a transparent record of your growth, attracts collaborators, and builds trust with a potential audience before a product is finished.

**Mental Model:** Building in public is the opposite of the "big reveal" product launch — instead of hiding until perfect, you invite people to watch and react at every step. The audience becomes advisors, early users, and advocates before you even finish.

**Common Misconceptions:**
- Building in public requires a large existing audience — sharing in relevant communities (Discord, Subreddits, Hacker News) gives immediate reach to people who care about what you're building, regardless of your follower count.
- You must share everything, including all failures — selective transparency is fine; share what is genuinely useful or interesting to your audience, not every keystroke.

**Interview Skeleton:**
- What it is: the practice of sharing progress on AI projects openly and incrementally as you build, rather than only at completion
- Why it matters: builds audience, accountability, and feedback loops that improve both the product and your learning speed
- Example: describe a one-month build-in-public plan for an AI engineering side project, including what you'd share, where, and how often
